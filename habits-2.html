Core Principles
The Science of Habit Formation
01
Small Actions Matter
A 1% improvement every day compounds to 37x better over a year. The scale of the action is irrelevant — the consistency is everything. Start with something so small failure feels embarrassing.
02
Consistency Wins
Five imperfect days per week every week for a year outperforms seven perfect days per week for three weeks. Habits are built by showing up, not by performing perfectly.
03
Systems Beat Goals
Goals tell you where you want to go. Systems determine whether you get there. The person who builds a reading system reads. The person who sets a reading goal eventually stops.
04
Identity Shapes Behaviour
The most lasting habit change starts with identity. "I am someone who reads" is more powerful than "I want to read more." Every action is a vote for the person you are becoming.
The Roadmap
5 Steps to Building Lasting Habits
1
Choose One Habit
Pick one small, specific habit. Not "exercise more" — but "walk for 10 minutes after breakfast." Clarity removes the friction of deciding.
2
Make It Easy
Reduce friction to zero. Lay out your clothes the night before. Open the book and leave it on your pillow. The easier the behaviour, the more likely you repeat it.
3
Stay Consistent
Perform the habit every day for the first 30 days — even a minimal version. "Never miss twice" is the most important rule in early habit formation.
4
Track Progress
Mark each completed day on a calendar or habit tracker. The visual chain creates a motivational pull that makes missing feel worse than doing.
5
Stack Habits
After the first habit is automatic, add a second using habit stacking: "After I do X, I will do Y." Piggyback new behaviours onto established ones to build compound routines.

Avoid These
Common Habit Mistakes
Starting too big
Committing to a one-hour workout when you've never trained consistently guarantees failure within two weeks. Start with five minutes. The habit of showing up is the skill — intensity comes later.
Skipping tracking
Without a visual record, your brain has no feedback loop. Tracking transforms abstract behaviour into concrete data and creates the streak pressure that keeps you showing up on low-motivation days.
Changing habits constantly
Switching habits before they're automated produces zero lasting behaviour change. A habit needs 21–66 days of consistent repetition before it becomes automatic. Change after it's locked, not before.
Seeking perfection
Waiting for the perfect conditions, the perfect routine, or the perfect time is procrastination wearing a planning costume. An imperfect habit done consistently beats a perfect plan done once.
Quitting too early
The first three weeks are the hardest — the behaviour is effortful, results are invisible, and motivation is low. Most people quit here. The automaticity that makes habits easy comes at week four and beyond.
Too many habits at once
Launching ten new habits simultaneously splits willpower across all of them and guarantees most fail. Build one habit fully before adding the next. Depth before breadth — always.
